Tend a newborn star in the corner of your desktop. Gather light, raise worlds and guide their people from the first fire to the stars. Collapse your sun in a supernova, decipher a lost alien alphabet, and, at the very end, tear open a black hole into the multiverse.

A star on your desktop

Sunkeeper is built to sit in the corner of your screen and keep running while you do something else. It is an idle game in the honest sense: it asks for a glance, a decision, and a little patience, and it keeps its own time when you look away.

What you are tending is a star. It begins newborn and small, and everything that follows — every world, every civilisation, every ending — comes from the light it makes.

Gather light

Light is the first resource and the root of all the others. You gather it, you spend it, and you learn to gather it faster. That loop is the spine of the game, and the rest of Sunkeeper grows out of it rather than sitting beside it.

Raise worlds, guide their people

Light turns into worlds, and worlds turn into people. You guide them from the first fire to the stars — the whole climb, from the first thing they learn to make to the moment they leave the system that made them.

You are not one of them. You are the thing in the sky they build their calendars around.

Collapse the sun

Every star ends, and yours ends in a supernova. Collapsing it is something you choose and something you aim for — the hinge the long game turns on, rather than a failure state to avoid.

Decipher a lost alphabet

Somewhere in all of this is a lost alien alphabet, and it is meant to be genuinely deciphered rather than unlocked. It is the part of Sunkeeper that rewards paying attention to something other than the numbers.

The last door

And at the very end, you tear open a black hole into the multiverse. That is where Sunkeeper is going. What it opens onto is the thing worth arriving at on your own.

Where it is

Sunkeeper is in development for Steam, targeting a Q1 2027 release. The store page is live now, and a wishlist there is the single most useful thing you can do for a small game before launch — it is most of what Steam looks at when it decides who gets shown the game on release day.
